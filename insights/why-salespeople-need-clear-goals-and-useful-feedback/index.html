Why salespeople need clear goals and useful feedback—not just more pressure

A quota tells sellers the result you want. Research helps explain the goals, feedback and coaching that should sit behind it.

By Jeff Cohen · The Floor · M.S. Industrial-Organizational Psychology

You can increase a sales target, ask for more activity and remind the team that the quarter matters. None of those actions tells a seller which opportunity deserves attention, what to improve in discovery or how to handle a stalled decision.

For a founder managing a small B2B team, the useful question is: Does each seller have a clear goal, usable feedback and someone helping them decide what to change?

Four peer-reviewed papers offer a way to think about that job. Two examine sales performance directly; two draw on broader performance research. Their findings support more precise management, not a promise that a particular meeting schedule will increase revenue.

1. A target needs a strategy and feedback

In their 2002 review in American Psychologist, Edwin Locke and Gary Latham described evidence that specific, challenging goals can outperform vague instructions to try harder. The conditions matter: commitment, relevant ability and knowledge, feedback about progress, and task complexity influence how goals work. For unfamiliar, complex work, a learning goal can be more useful than an outcome target alone. [1]

This is general task-performance research. It does not validate any particular sales quota, activity target or pipeline-coverage rule.

Our application: keep the revenue target, then agree on the work and learning required to pursue it. A rep entering a new buyer category might need to test discovery questions and understand the buying process before simply being told to schedule more demos.

Illustrative management conversation

“Our revenue target stays the same. For these opportunities, identify who approves the purchase, what has to happen before a decision and the next action agreed with the buyer. Bring the gaps to our review so we can decide what to do.”

The example is a management recommendation, not a script tested in the cited studies.

2. More feedback is not automatically better feedback

Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi’s 1996 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin found that feedback improved performance on average, but more than one-third of the feedback interventions reduced performance. Their analysis suggested that effectiveness declined as attention shifted away from the task and toward the self. [2]

The research spans different tasks and settings. It does not mean one-third of sales coaching conversations fail, or that difficult feedback should be avoided.

Our application: discuss observable work before making judgments about the person. “You are not hungry enough” gives a seller little to act on. A review of a specific call or opportunity can identify what was missed and what to try next.

Illustrative feedback

“The buyer explained the problem, but we did not establish how they will decide or who else is involved. What would you ask next time? Let’s work through that opening, then review what happens on the next relevant call.”

That still allows a direct performance conversation. The manager names the gap, asks the seller to think and follows up on the agreed change.

3. Unclear expectations are a sales-performance issue

Willem Verbeke, Bart Dietz and Ernst Verwaal’s sales-performance meta-analysis, published in the 2011 volume of the Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, synthesized research from 1982–2008. Role ambiguity was negatively associated with sales performance. Selling knowledge and adaptability were also relevant predictors, and relationships varied by context and measurement. [3]

These associations do not prove that clarifying a role will cause a specific revenue increase. They do give managers reason to inspect expectations before attributing every shortfall to motivation.

Our application: ask whether a seller can answer the following questions without guessing:

  • Which accounts and opportunities take priority?
  • What information makes an opportunity qualified?
  • Who owns the next customer action?
  • Which decisions can the seller make independently?
  • When should the manager or founder step in?

If ownership changes depending on who last spoke to the founder, another reminder about quota will not resolve that uncertainty. Write down the decisions and revisit them when the work changes.

4. Coaching skill matters more than filling the calendar

A study by Jason Dahling and colleagues in Personnel Psychology examined 1,246 sales representatives in 136 teams within a pharmaceutical organization over a year. Managers’ coaching skill was associated with annual sales goal attainment, with team role clarity explaining part of the relationship. More frequent coaching was negatively related to attainment when coaching skill was low. [4]

This was a predictive field study in one organization, not a randomized trial across founder-led B2B companies. It cannot establish that adding coaching meetings will improve your team.

Our application: evaluate what a coaching conversation produces. Does the seller leave with a clearer understanding, a useful decision or a skill to practice? Is that change revisited? A full calendar is not evidence of effective coaching.

A practical weekly check for a small sales team

The following is The Floor’s suggested starting point, informed by the research above. The papers do not test this checklist or prescribe this cadence.

Management jobQuestion to answerUseful output
PrioritiesWhat matters most this week?A short list of opportunities and actions with owners.
Pipeline decisionsWhat changed, and what evidence supports the next step?An updated view of the deal and a dated action.
Seller coachingWhat specific part of the seller’s approach needs attention?One behavior or skill to practice and revisit.
Role clarityWhich decision or responsibility is still ambiguous?A named owner and clear escalation rule.
Follow-throughWhat happened after the previous commitment?A decision to continue, adjust or address a missed standard.

Keep the rhythm proportionate to the sales cycle. A small team does not need a meeting for every question. It does need someone accountable for keeping the management work consistent.

Where AI can help the manager

AI can help organize approved call notes, identify missing CRM fields or prepare questions for a review. Treat those outputs as preparation that requires verification. The cited papers do not evaluate AI-assisted sales management; this is The Floor’s operating recommendation.

The manager still owns the judgment: whether a forecast is credible, whether a seller needs coaching or a performance conversation, and which customer issue deserves leadership attention. A fluent summary should not turn missing buyer evidence into a confident close date.

What this evidence does—and does not—say

The research gives us grounds to examine goals, feedback, expectations and coaching quality. It does not establish that every struggling sales team has a management problem. Product fit, territory, lead quality, pricing and individual capability also deserve investigation.

These papers were published between 1996 and 2016. They provide established foundations, not a claim to cover every recent development in sales or AI. The practical examples here are interpretations, not validated treatment protocols or measured client results.

For the founder, the decision remains concrete: Who is doing this management work now, and can they do it consistently?
